The Hebrew years in 2028

Gregorian 2028 is spread over two Hebrew years. Until Rosh Hashanah it is the Hebrew year 5788, which is an ordinary year of twelve months; from Rosh Hashanah it is 5789, which is an ordinary year of twelve months. This matters because a leap year has an extra month, Adar II, and in a leap year Purim is kept in Adar II and Passover comes a month later in the Gregorian calendar.

The autumn holidays

Rosh Hashanah, the New Year, begins at sundown on the evening before Thursday 21 September 2028. Yom Kippur follows 9 days later, on Saturday 30 September 2028, and Sukkot begins on Thursday 5 October 2028. These are the ten days between the New Year and the Day of Atonement, a time of reflection and, for many, of asking forgiveness of the people they have wronged before asking it of God. Rosh Hashanah never falls on a Sunday, Wednesday or Friday in the standard Hebrew calendar, which is one of the rules built into the arithmetic; in 2028 it is on a Thursday.

The spring holidays

Purim falls on Sunday 12 March 2028 and Passover begins at sundown before Tuesday 11 April 2028, which is 30 days later. A Passover seder is held on the first night (and in the diaspora also on the second), and the festival lasts seven days in Israel and eight elsewhere. Shavuot comes seven weeks after the second night of Passover, on Wednesday 31 May 2028.

The summer fasts

Between Shavuot and Rosh Hashanah come two fasts that remember the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem: the Fast of 17 Tammuz and Tisha B’Av, which in 2028 is on Tuesday 1 August 2028. Tisha B’Av is the saddest day of the Jewish year; it is a full fast from sundown to nightfall, and many people sit on low stools and read the Book of Lamentations. If the day falls on Shabbat the fast is moved to Sunday.

Using this page

Check the dates with your own synagogue or rabbi before you plan anything that depends on them, since communities differ on details such as the second day of a festival and the start of the evening. If you work with Jewish colleagues or students, it is considerate to avoid scheduling exams or deadlines on the first and last days of Passover, Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Sukkot and Shavuot, which are days of rest, and on Fridays near sundown in winter.

Jewish holidays begin at sundown on the evening before the date shown. Dates follow the standard Hebrew calendar; communities outside Israel keep a second day of several festivals. Customs differ between Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and other communities, and this is not a ruling: ask your rabbi.
