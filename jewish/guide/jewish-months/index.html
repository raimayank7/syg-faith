Why there are thirteen months

Each Hebrew month begins with the new moon and lasts 29 or 30 days, so twelve months make about 354 days, about eleven days fewer than a solar year. To keep festivals in their seasons, Passover in spring and Sukkot in autumn, a thirteenth month is added in seven years of every nineteen. That month is Adar I; the ordinary Adar becomes Adar II, and Purim is kept in Adar II. The count of years shows this: a Hebrew year whose number leaves a remainder of 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 or 0 when divided by 19 is a leap year.

Two ways of counting the year

The months are listed here from Nisan, which the Torah calls the first month and in which Passover falls. The Jewish New Year, however, is on 1 Tishrei, six months later, and the year number changes then. So Rosh Hashanah starts a new year number while Nisan, six months afterwards, is still counted in the same year. For everyday dates people use Tishrei as the beginning, and a Hebrew birthday follows the month and day of birth, which is why leap years need care.

Reading the table

The Gregorian months in the second column are only a guide, since each Hebrew month begins on a different Gregorian date every year and the overlap can shift by up to a month. For exact dates, use the converter on the home page or the year pages, and ask a rabbi about customs.

Jewish holidays begin at sundown on the evening before the date shown. Dates follow the standard Hebrew calendar; communities outside Israel keep a second day of several festivals. Customs differ between Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and other communities, and this is not a ruling: ask your rabbi.
