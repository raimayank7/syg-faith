The 12 books

Every book of the Meditations.

The Meditations were written by the emperor for himself, in Greek, in the last years of his life (he died in 180 CE). The twelve books are not ordered by theme; they are the notes of a diary. Open one and read a few sections.

The Meditations is a private notebook, not a book written for readers. The Greek title usually given to it means "to himself", and the emperor repeats his reminders because he needed to hear them again: be patient with difficult people, do the work in front of you, remember that life is short, and judge things by how you respond rather than by how they look. Book 1 is different from the rest: it is a list of the people he learned from, with the single thing he took from each of them. Because he wrote in the middle of public duties and long military campaigns, many passages are short, and the same thoughts return in different words, which is why the longer books (4 to 9) are good to dip into rather than read straight through.

How to use this page: the table below shows how many numbered sections each book has. A book of 16 or 17 sections can be read in one sitting; the longer ones are better taken a few sections at a time, perhaps one in the morning and one in the evening. If you only have a few minutes, the topic pages collect passages on anger, death, calm and duty. The English text here is the 1862 translation by George Long, which is in the public domain; some of its phrases sound old-fashioned, and you will see the notes beside it in plain modern English.
