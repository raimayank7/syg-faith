The Noble Eightfold Path

The eight parts of the Buddha's path: right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration, grouped into wisdom, ethics and meditation.

The Eightfold Path is the Buddha's practical guide to ending suffering. It is not eight steps taken in order; the parts support one another.

GroupPartIn plain words
WisdomRight viewSeeing things as they are: the Four Noble Truths
WisdomRight intentionA mind of goodwill, renunciation and harmlessness
EthicsRight speechNo lying, harsh words, gossip or idle chatter
EthicsRight actionNo killing, stealing or harmful conduct
EthicsRight livelihoodEarning a living without harming others
MeditationRight effortPreventing harmful states and developing good ones
MeditationRight mindfulnessBeing aware of body, feelings, mind and mental states
MeditationRight concentrationSteadying the mind in meditation

Chapter 20 of the Dhammapada, "The Way", speaks of this path.

The three groups

Tradition arranges the eight parts into three groups. Wisdom (right view and right intention) is about understanding and motive: seeing the world as the Four Noble Truths describe it, and wanting what is kind. Ethics (right speech, action and livelihood) is how that understanding shows in daily life: what you say, what you do, and how you earn your living. Meditation (right effort, mindfulness and concentration) is the training of the mind that makes the other two steady.

Not a ladder

The parts are often listed in order, but they are not climbed like steps. A person may begin with kind speech and honest work, find that their mind is calmer and their view clearer, and then take up meditation. Each part helps the others. The word translated as “right” is in the original closer to “complete” or “well-formed”; it is not a moral ranking, but a way of saying a part is done skilfully.

Where to begin

Many teachers suggest beginning with the simplest practices: telling the truth, refusing to speak harshly, and paying attention for a few minutes each day. The Dhammapada verses on this site, especially the chapter called “The Way”, can be read slowly alongside the table. This page is a plain introduction and not a substitute for a teacher; if you want to practise, a local Buddhist community can help.
