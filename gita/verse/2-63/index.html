Bhagavad Gita 2.63

Chapter 2, verse 63: the Sanskrit, the Roman letters, the meaning and what it is used for.

2.63

क्रोधाद्भवति संमोहः संमोहात्स्मृतिविभ्रमः
स्मृतिभ्रंशाद्बुद्धिनाशो बुद्धिनाशात्प्रणश्यति

krodhādbhavati saṃmohaḥ saṃmohātsmṛtivibhramaḥ
smṛtibhraṃśādbuddhināśo buddhināśātpraṇaśyati

Meaning

From anger comes delusion; from delusion, loss of memory; from loss of memory, the destruction of the understanding; and from the destruction of understanding the person is ruined.

Explanation

The chain continues. Anger clouds judgement, one forgets what one stood for, reason fails and the person acts against his own good. Anyone who has said something in anger that he later regretted has seen a small version of the whole chain. The verse is often used in schools and by counsellors as a map of how a bad decision is made.

Where it fits

This is verse 63 of 72 in chapter 2, "The wisdom of the self and of steady action" (Sankhya Yoga). previous verse (2.62) · next verse (2.64)

The chapter around this verse

Krishna begins to teach. He first says that grief for what is unavoidable is misplaced, because the self is not born and does not die; the body changes as clothes do. Then he turns to action: do your duty without clinging to the result, with the same mind in success and failure. The chapter closes with the portrait of the sthitaprajna, the person of steady wisdom, who is not shaken by pleasure or pain, and the famous chain that leads from brooding on objects to attachment, anger, confusion and ruin. Many people who read only one chapter read this one.

Other verses of chapter 2 that have a page of their own on this site: 2.13, 2.14, 2.20, 2.22, 2.27, 2.38, 2.47, 2.48, 2.62.

Chapter 2 is called The wisdom of the self and of steady action (Sankhya Yoga) and has 72 verses. This verse is number 63, so it comes about 88% of the way through. You can read the whole chapter, verse by verse in Sanskrit and Roman letters, on its chapter page.

Gita verses on anger and desire

The Gita gives a short map of how a mind goes wrong: brooding, attachment, desire, anger, confusion, loss of memory, loss of judgement (2.62-63). It names desire, anger and greed as three gates to self-ruin (16.21). It also describes the person who neither disturbs the world nor is disturbed by it (12.15).

Gita verses on controlling the mind

Arjuna says the mind is as restless as the wind; Krishna agrees and gives the remedy of practice and detachment (6.35). The self is both our friend and our enemy (6.5, 6.6), and moderation in eating, sleeping, work and leisure is the base of calm (6.17).

A reminder when reading any single verse: it was spoken as part of a long conversation, so the verses before and after it (shown above) change how it is heard. The explanation here is our own wording; teachers in different traditions explain it differently.
