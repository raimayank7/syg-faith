The 18 chapters

Every chapter with its Sanskrit name and a note on what it teaches.

The 18 chapters follow Arjuna from despair (chapter 1) to the final teaching of surrender (chapter 18). Tradition groups them as six chapters on action, six on devotion and six on knowledge, though each chapter mixes the three. Choose a chapter to read its verses.

What each chapter teaches

Chapter 1: Arjuna's despair (Arjuna Vishada Yoga)

The armies stand ready at Kurukshetra. Dhritarashtra asks Sanjaya what his sons and the Pandavas are doing, and Sanjaya describes the two armies and their heroes. Arjuna asks Krishna, his charioteer, to drive between the armies. Seeing teachers, uncles and cousins on both sides, he is overcome by grief: his limbs fail, his bow slips, his mind reels. He says he sees no good in killing his own people and sits down in the chariot. The chapter is the setting for all that follows: a capable person, frozen at the moment of a hard duty, who has to ask for guidance.

Chapter 2: The wisdom of the self and of steady action (Sankhya Yoga)

Krishna begins to teach. He first says that grief for what is unavoidable is misplaced, because the self is not born and does not die; the body changes as clothes do. Then he turns to action: do your duty without clinging to the result, with the same mind in success and failure. The chapter closes with the portrait of the sthitaprajna, the person of steady wisdom, who is not shaken by pleasure or pain, and the famous chain that leads from brooding on objects to attachment, anger, confusion and ruin. Many people who read only one chapter read this one.

Chapter 3: The path of action (Karma Yoga)

Arjuna asks why Krishna urges him to act if knowledge is better than action. Krishna answers that no one can stay without action even for a moment, so the question is how to act. Work done as an offering, without selfish craving, frees a person; work done only for oneself binds. Leaders set the example that others follow. Desire and anger are named as the enemies that push people into wrong action, and the senses and mind must be brought under control by the understanding. The chapter is the basis of what many call karma yoga, the discipline of selfless work.

Chapter 4: Knowledge, action and renunciation (Jnana Karma Sannyasa Yoga)

Krishna says this teaching was given to ancient teachers and had been lost over time. He explains that whenever righteousness declines he takes birth, and that his birth and works are divine. The chapter looks at the different kinds of sacrifice (yajna), from gifts to study to breath control, and says that the sacrifice of knowledge is better than that of material things. It ends with the praise of knowledge as the greatest purifier and with advice to ask teachers with respect, serve them and question them.

Chapter 5: Renunciation and action (Karma Sannyasa Yoga)

Arjuna asks which is better, renunciation of action or its practice. Krishna says both lead to the highest, but action done with detachment is easier than renunciation without it, and the two are not truly different when understood. The person who offers all actions to the Supreme and acts without attachment is not stained by sin, like a lotus leaf in water. The wise see the same self in a learned person, a cow, an elephant, a dog and an outcaste. The chapter ends with the peace that comes to the one who has mastered desire, fear and anger.

Chapter 6: The yoga of meditation (Dhyana Yoga)

Krishna describes the practice of meditation: a clean place, a steady seat, a mind gathered in one point. He stresses moderation in eating, sleeping and effort, and says the mind can be uplifted by itself and can ruin itself. Arjuna admits that the mind is restless and as hard to control as the wind; Krishna agrees but says practice and detachment can steady it. The chapter closes with the question of what happens to a sincere person whose yoga is incomplete, and the assurance that no good effort is lost.

Chapter 7: Knowledge and its realisation (Jnana Vijnana Yoga)

Krishna begins to describe his own nature: the lower nature of earth, water, fire, air, space, mind, intellect and ego, and the higher nature that sustains the world. Everything is strung on him like pearls on a thread. His divine power (maya) is hard to cross, but those who turn to him cross it. Four kinds of people worship him: the distressed, the seeker of knowledge, the seeker of gain and the wise; the wise one who is steadfast in love is dearest. People who worship other gods with desire for results get what they ask, in the limited form it takes.

Chapter 8: The imperishable (Akshara Brahma Yoga)

Arjuna asks about Brahman, the self, action and the divine realms. Krishna answers briefly, then speaks of the moment of death: whatever a person remembers at the end, that state they reach, so the mind should be trained to remember the Supreme throughout life. He describes the cycle of creation, the day and night of Brahma, and the two paths that souls may take after death, the path of light and the path of darkness. The chapter advises steady remembrance together with doing one's duty.

Chapter 9: The royal knowledge and royal secret (Raja Vidya Raja Guhya Yoga)

Krishna calls this the most confidential knowledge, easy to practise and giving results at once. The whole universe is pervaded by him in an unmanifest form; beings rest in him, yet he does not rest in them. Even a leaf, flower, fruit or water offered with love is accepted. He declares that he is equal to all beings, that even a person of bad conduct who worships him with undivided love should be seen as good, because that person soon becomes righteous. He promises to provide and protect for those who are constantly devoted.

Chapter 10: The divine glories (Vibhuti Yoga)

At Arjuna's request Krishna lists his manifestations: among the sages he is Vyasa, among the stars the moon, among the Vedas the Sama Veda, among the senses the mind, among the rivers the Ganga, among the mountains Meru, among letters the letter A. The chapter ends with the instruction that wherever there is glory, splendour or power, it comes from a fragment of his energy. It is a chapter of devotion and of praise, and it prepares the ground for the next one.

Chapter 11: The vision of the universal form (Vishvarupa Darshana Yoga)

Arjuna asks to see the cosmic form. Krishna gives him a divine eye, and Arjuna sees the whole universe in the body of the Lord: countless faces, eyes and arms, the gods, the sages, and the armies rushing into the mouths of the form like rivers into the sea. Terrified, he asks who it is; the answer is that it is Time, the destroyer of worlds, and that the warriors are already slain by it, so he should be only the instrument. Arjuna begs to see the gentle human form again, and Krishna returns to it, saying only the one devoted with undivided love can see him in this way.

Chapter 12: The yoga of devotion (Bhakti Yoga)

Arjuna asks whether those who worship the personal form or the unmanifest are better. Krishna says those who fix their mind on him with faith are the best, but the path to the unmanifest is difficult for embodied beings. He gives steps for those who cannot do the highest: practice, then work for him, then give up the fruit of action. The second half is a famous portrait of the devotee who is dear to him: free from hatred, friendly and compassionate, free from possessiveness and ego, equal in pleasure and pain, forgiving, content, and who neither troubles the world nor is troubled by it.

Chapter 13: The field and the knower of the field (Kshetra Kshetrajna Vibhaga Yoga)

The body is called the field (kshetra) and the one who knows it is the knower of the field (kshetrajna). Krishna lists the qualities that make up knowledge, from humility and non-violence to steadiness and equanimity, and describes what is to be known, which is beyond beginning and without qualities. Nature (prakriti) and the person (purusha) are both without beginning. The one who sees the Supreme equally present in all beings, not perishing when they perish, sees truly.

Chapter 14: The three qualities (Gunatraya Vibhaga Yoga)

Nature has three gunas, sattva (clarity), rajas (passion) and tamas (inertia), which bind the embodied self. Krishna describes what each does: sattva binds by attachment to happiness and knowledge, rajas by attachment to action, tamas by confusion and sloth. They compete in a person at different times. A person who sees that only the gunas act, who is equal in pleasure and pain, praise and blame, and who has crossed beyond the three, attains the highest.

Chapter 15: The supreme person (Purushottama Yoga)

The chapter opens with the image of the inverted ashvattha tree, with roots above and branches below, whose leaves are the Vedas. The wise cut it with the sword of detachment and seek the state from which there is no return. Krishna describes how the same light that is in the sun and fire is in him, how he enters the earth to support all beings, how he abides in the heart of all as memory and knowledge. He names himself Purushottama, the highest person, beyond the perishable and imperishable.

Chapter 16: Divine and demonic qualities (Daivasura Sampad Vibhaga Yoga)

Krishna lists the divine qualities: fearlessness, purity of heart, charity, self-control, truthfulness, non-violence, compassion, gentleness and more, and the demonic ones: pride, arrogance, anger, harshness and ignorance. People of the demonic type deny truth, consider the world as born of desire, pursue wealth and power by unfair means and think "I am the lord". The three gates of hell are lust, anger and greed, and they must be given up. The scriptures are to be the guide in deciding what to do and what not to do.

Chapter 17: The three kinds of faith (Shraddhatraya Vibhaga Yoga)

Faith, too, is of three kinds according to a person's nature. Krishna describes how the three gunas colour food, sacrifice, austerity and charity. Austerity of the body, speech and mind is described in turn: respect for teachers, cleanliness, non-violence; truthful, pleasant and beneficial speech; calmness, silence, self-control. The chapter ends with the meaning of the syllables Om, Tat and Sat, and the statement that any act done without faith is "asat", of no worth here or hereafter.

Chapter 18: Liberation through renunciation (Moksha Sannyasa Yoga)

The longest chapter sums up the teaching. Krishna explains renunciation and giving up, the five causes of action, and how knowledge, action, doer, understanding, steadiness and happiness each have three kinds according to the gunas. Duties are divided by nature and one finds perfection by doing one's own work as worship. The chapter reaches its peak in verse 66, "abandon all dharmas and take refuge in me alone", and ends with Sanjaya's report that he heard this wonderful dialogue and with his conviction that victory is wherever Krishna and Arjuna are.
