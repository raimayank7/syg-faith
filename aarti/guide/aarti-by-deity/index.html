Aarti by deity

The best-known aarti for each deity, the day and the festival.

This table lists the aarti that is best known for each deity. Different regions and families sing other aartis too; the day column is the common custom for that deity, and does not apply everywhere. The words are not printed on this site.

How to use the table

Find the deity you want to worship in the first column. The second column gives the day of the week that is usually kept for that deity, the third names the aarti that most people know best, and the fourth names the main festival on which it is sung with special devotion. If you are not sure which aarti to learn, begin with the one that is shown for the deity your family already worships; most families know one or two, and a short aarti is easier to learn by hearing it than by reading it.

Why aartis differ

The same deity can have many aartis, because different regions, communities and temples have each composed their own. A well-known example is the aarti to Ganesha, which is sung in one form in Maharashtra and in another in north India. The aarti listed here is the one most widely used, not the only correct one. If your family or temple sings another, sing that.

Words and tunes

We describe the aartis and do not print their words, because several are still under copyright in particular recordings and printed versions, and a printed aarti book is the best place to read the words. Many aartis follow a simple pattern, a refrain that repeats after each verse, so people who have never read the words can join in after a few lines. For the steps of the ritual see the guide to doing aarti, and for the stories and symbols of each deity see Gods and Goddesses.

This page describes common practice in plain words. Families and temples differ, and your own tradition comes first. We do not print the words of the aartis and chalisas here; use a printed book from your family or temple for the exact text.
